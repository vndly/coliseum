# Game

## Match

- **Match authority**: Every client is trusted; there is no server. The player whose turn it is simulates their own throw, and what they write when the dice stop is authoritative — every other player teleports their bodies onto it, which is what stops two simulations drifting apart over a match. `Die.teleport` is the one deliberate exception to the rule that only the body writes to the meshes; nothing else may write to a body from outside the scene. Nothing may write a throw the scene cannot run, either: the controls stay shut until `DishScene` reports its physics world started, because a throw is written to the match before its dice come to rest and a scene with no world never brings them to rest — the hand is charged and the turn moves nowhere. And a throw is shown here before it is written, so a write that is refused takes its dice back out again; left standing they are dice no other player has, and the retry is given the same sequence number and so the very same identifiers. The number is claimed rather than assumed, which is why `submitThrow` is a transaction: anonymous sign-in is shared across an origin, so one match open in two tabs is the same seated player twice and both reach for the same number off the same cached match. Written blind, the second overwrote the first and the table then animated one throw while the bowl it was judged against came from the other.
- **Reading a stored match**: `parseMatchState` is the boundary, and everything it reads is refused rather than repaired. `groupSize` is the one field with an answer for absence rather than a refusal, and for the same reason `bot` has one: matches written before the lobby offered the choice say nothing at all, and every one of them was played at two. Present and not a size the game offers is refused like anything else. `flush` beside it is a boolean and is read the way `bot` and `hasThrown` are — what the document says, or what it does not — rather than refused; the default is only turned around, to on, because every match written before the choice existed played the flush. Anything that becomes a length or a body count is bounded against `MATCH_DICE` — the dice the match was dealt plus the opening die, which no hand paid for and which is what lets a winning hand be one larger than the deal — anything that becomes an index is bounded against the list it points into, and every sequence number and every version is a whole number that cannot be negative — a turn pointing at a seat nobody is in is a turn nothing can move on again, and a verdict claiming a throw the match has never heard of reads as judged to every guard that asks it and as unjudged to the screen. A read that is not a match at all is answered rather than dropped, and the three of them are not the same thing: no document at that code, a document this game cannot read, and a listener the store has torn down. Held on to, the last state that did read leaves the rail, the turn and the throw controls live over a match that is not there. Every stored attitude has to be a unit quaternion, because Rapier builds a body from one that is not and then never moves it or puts it to sleep, which holds the whole table unsettled. `verdict` is read as strictly as the bowl beside it despite being the replay script: `judged` on the screen, and the idempotency guards in both `submitVerdict` and the takeover, all read it as the record of what has been judged, so a verdict that will not parse would stop every turn and let the next takeover pay a throw's winnings twice.
- **Rules of play**: The whole ruleset lives in `src/match/rules.ts` as pure functions — add rules there, not in the client or the screen. It is said a second time in prose, in `src/components/rules_sheet.vue`, which is the sheet both the lobby and the match open: the one place in the app that has to be answered by hand when a rule here changes. It says nothing a match settles for itself — the size of a group, whether the flush is played — because the same sheet is opened from a screen where no match exists yet and from one where a match does, and a sheet that named this match's answer would be two different sheets. Those two answers are given instead by the placard in the match screen's upper-left corner, opened from a button of its own beside the one the sheet is opened from — the two share a fitting, the way the audio switches share theirs, because both say something about the table rather than alter it: both answers are settled when the match is made and never written again, and neither can be read off the bowl, so a player who joined by code has nowhere else to learn what this table plays by. The group's size is stamped there as a figure rather than as dice, the way the rail says a hand: dice on a plate over the table would read as dice in play, and the lobby draws the same number as dice because there it is the choice rather than the answer. It is read once and then in the way, so it stands only while the button is toggled on, and the press that opened it puts it away. Both the button and the plate wait for the seats to fill, which the switches beside them do not: the corner is drawn over the card that waits for them, and that card is centred and grows with every seat, so on a phone it reaches up far enough for the plate to stand across the match code. What the rail across the top holds itself clear of is the widest thing in that corner — the fittings side by side until they stack, and the placard from there down, with the room kept whether the plate is out or not, since the press that opens it is not a press the rail can be asked to reflow for. The corner passes the pointer on and the fittings take it back, as the chrome opposite does with its own buttons, because the table is behind all of it and the plate is a thing to read. A settled bowl is judged once, by whoever gets there first: the thrower does it as soon as their dice stop, and if they leave without managing it the next player judges it from their own simulation after a timeout. That offer is made again if it is not taken — the first is the next player's alone, so that five tables do not reach for one bowl, and every one after it is open to anybody whose own scene ran the throw, because the seat asked first can be the seat that has gone. The transaction makes a second attempt harmless, and the alternative is worse than a wasted write: a bowl nobody judges is a match in which nobody can throw or pass ever again, with the thrower's hand still charged for dice it never got back. `MatchClient.submitVerdict` is that one write, and it is a transaction because more than one player can attempt it. It carries the whole outcome at once — the bowl, every hand, whose turn it is, and the winner — plus a `verdict` replay script every player animates from. `bowlVersion` still counts writes to the bowl and is what tells a client the verdict is new.
- **Judging a bowl**: One settled bowl is read in a fixed order, and the order is the rule rather than an implementation detail: the sixes leave the match first, and only what is left is looked at — first for a flush, then for groups. So two sixes are not a pair, and a bowl reading one to five beside a six is still a flush, because by the time the flush is asked about the six is already out of the match. How many dice of one value make a group is the match's own — `groupSize`, chosen in the lobby off `GROUP_SIZES` and settled with the seats — rather than a constant in `rules.ts`, because each player judges their own bowl from the same functions and a table that disagrees about what a group is is a table that disagrees about who won. A flush is the whole bowl holding one of every value a die keeps — one 1, one 2, one 3, one 4, one 5 and nothing else — and the whole of it goes back to the hand that completed it. Whether a match plays it at all is the match's own too, beside `groupSize`: `flush` is chosen in the lobby and settled with the seats, and turned off nothing but a group or a run of sixes ever empties the bowl, so the all-in turns that follow an empty one grow rare with it. At a group of two that alone walks the bowl's ceiling from four dice to five, since all of one to five may now sit in it at once. It can never coexist with a group, since five different values share nothing, which is why the two are asked in turn rather than combined. It ends the turn exactly as a group does, and it empties the bowl, so the next player arrives at an empty one and goes all in. In the `verdict` replay a flush travels as ordinary `returned` dice: the scene animates it as it animates a pair, and nothing about the stored shape changes. A group of three is a different game rather than a harder one, and the flush is where it shows: pairs now stay in the bowl, so the bowl's ceiling goes from four dice to ten, and a flush still needs exactly five of them with no value repeated — which a bowl that is allowed to repeat one rarely manages.
- **Hands and elimination**: A die in the bowl belongs to nobody, so `players` carries identity alone, settled for good the moment the match starts, and a hand in `pools` is what its player is holding rather than who is owed it. It is a list all the same, and not a count: each entry is one die's colour, and `poolSize` is that list's length. An empty hand *is* elimination and needs no flag: nothing in the game can take a hand off empty, since it only grows by its own player pairing dice, which needs their turn, which is skipped.
- **Colours**: A colour is how a die looks and nothing else — paint for eight of the sixteen skins, and a material for the other eight, which the game treats identically. Every player picks one in the lobby, off the fixed palette in `src/scene/die_skins.ts`, and their six start in it — and go on starting in it: a seat is settled the moment the match starts, so until then `join` revises the name and the colour of a seat this player already holds and deals that hand again in the new paint, rather than returning as though there were nothing to do; from then on the paint travels with the die rather than with the hand, so a pair won off somebody arrives in their colour and is thrown on in it. That is the whole reason a hand is a list — a count could say how much a player holds and never what. Nothing in `rules.ts` reads a colour: it is carried, handed on, and never judged. Which die a mixed hand throws is decided by the queue, oldest first, because no rule turns on the answer and a player asked to pick paint mid-turn is being asked a question with no stakes.

  The palette is fixed rather than free because every swatch answers three things at the point it is drawn: it reads against the walnut and the baize, it stays clear of the ember and green a verdict washes a die in, and it carries its own pip colour — black pips on a claret die is a face nobody can count. A wash replaces the body outright and takes the pips to dark with it, since both wash colours are light. The one die nobody threw, the opening die, is bone, which is also the colour a player is given before they choose one; the two are deliberately the same, so no reserved "nobody" value has to exist. Two people may sit down in the same colour — a colour is a skin they chose for themselves, not a badge the match hands out — but bots are drawn apart from each other and from the player, since nobody chose theirs and the draw is the only thing that can make that table readable.

  The second half of the palette answers the same three questions as a material rather than as a colour: marble, steel, maple, ice, malachite, glass, pearl and carbon. Each is a `DieFinish`, which `Dice.buildFinish` turns into a `MeshPhysicalMaterial`, and six of the eight carry a map generated in `die_textures.ts` — four as their colour, one as the steel's polish and one as the thickness of the pearl's film. No texture file ships with this project: what a material is made of is a set of figures in `dimensions.ts`, and the swatch the interface shows it as is a CSS impression of the same thing written beside `surfaceCss` in `die_skins.ts`, since a gradient cannot refract or tumble. Two of them, glass and ice, are see-through, and three.js draws those by sampling a second render of the scene: one extra pass per frame whenever either is on the table, which is why `TRANSMISSION_RESOLUTION_SCALE` halves it and why `PAINTED_SKINS` holds bots to the paint. A material is a skin like any other everywhere else — one number across the network, washed over by a verdict and painted back exactly as a colour is.
- **Turn order**: The order of play is drawn at random, once, in the same write that fills the last seat and starts the match — `shuffledPlayers` in `rules.ts`, run by whoever takes that seat, like every other authority in the game. Drawing a seat as each player arrives would work just as evenly, but a player would learn their own place while the lobby was still filling, and the whole table is meant to find out at the same moment. Until the match starts, `players` is join order, which is what lets `open_matches.ts` name a lobby row after `players[0]`; from the start of the match that entry is merely whoever the draw put first.
- **Bots**: A match started with bots is an ordinary match, written straight into the `playing` phase. `MatchClient.create` is given the seats `createBots` drew — each one a `MatchPlayer` carrying `bot: true`, named from a fixed pool of six, and coloured from the painted eight in `die_skins.ts` — `PAINTED_SKINS`, not the whole palette — both drawn without replacement so no two bots at a table share either and neither takes what the player is using — and, since those seats fill the match on the spot, the same write does the work the last player to arrive would otherwise do: the opening die placed, the order of play drawn, the bowl counted once. Nothing has to say that no human may join, because nothing lets one: `join` refuses any match not in the `lobby` phase, and the lobby lists that phase alone, so a bot match is unlisted and unjoinable for free. What it costs instead is that one browser is now playing several seats, which is why `submitThrow` and `submitVerdict` are told which seat threw rather than assuming the browser's own. Several seats, but only ever one view of them: an origin's tabs share the identity those seats were taken under, so the match screen claims the bot seats with a lock named after the code, and only the view holding it draws a pause or makes a move for them. The second matters more than it looks: the contested branch in `submitVerdict` hands the turn on for a thrower who never judged their own bowl, and measured against the browser it would fire on every bot turn and take away the one choice a bot has. A bot match lives or dies with that tab, and the ~2 seconds a bot's dice are in the air is a window where closing it strands the match for good — the throw is written, the verdict never is, and a reopened tab skips throws that were already in the match when it arrived. The window is small, only one person can reach it, and the answer is to start another match.
- **Bot strategy**: `nextBotMove` in `bots.ts`, and it is a smaller decision than it sounds — the other decision a bot makes, what it says about a throw, is `nextBotEmote` beside it and is described under **Emotes** below. A player may only pass once they have thrown, so the first throw of every turn is forced and the only choice a bot has is whether to throw *again* after one that paired nothing. What it decides against is the bowl, and the bowl is never large: every verdict takes out the sixes and then every group, so no value is ever sitting in it having already made one. That makes one throw countable, and it is counted rather than looked up in a table, because the size of a group is the match's own. Of the six faces the die can land on, one takes it out of the match, `payingFaces` counts the values the bowl holds one short of a group — each paying the whole group for the one die thrown — and, in a match that plays the flush, the one value that would complete one when the bowl is four distinct dice, paying the whole bowl; every other face leaves the die in the bowl, which costs the hand that die just as surely. At a group of two that comes to -0.67 against a bowl of one, -0.33 against two, level against three and +1.17 against four, which is the rule the constants used to state outright; at a group of three the bowl is larger and a group pays double, and the throw comes level once two values are sitting doubled in it. Without the flush the same count holds and the bowl simply goes a die further: five distinct values pay +0.67, the six being the only face left that does not. Level is taken rather than declined: a die that settles instead walks the bowl nearer a group, where the next throw is in the thrower's favour and the turn is still theirs. The one exception is a hand of one, where a throw that pairs nothing is elimination and there is no way back off zero — that die is risked only on a bowl where every face but the six pays, rather than on one that merely pays on average. The count ignores what the arriving die does to the dice already in the bowl, which can turn a face and is not worth modelling; it cuts both ways.
- **Open matches**: The lobby lists every match still in the `lobby` phase and made less than five minutes ago, and a row pressed takes the same seat the code field takes. Every match is listed, so a code is a convenience rather than a gate — a stranger can sit down in one without ever being told it. This is the one query in the game that reads the `matches` collection rather than a document by name, and it needs two things nothing else does: an `allow list` rule on `/matches/{code}`, and a composite index on `phase` and `createdAt`. The index is declared in `../backend/firestore.indexes.json` and is the one thing this repo says about Firestore itself; the rules are still console-only and are not reproducible from here. Without either, the listener fails and the card simply never appears, which is why no player is ever shown a word about it. The error goes to the console rather than the screen, though that is worth checking rather than trusting — a missing index has been observed reaching the browser with nothing logged at all, and the query that proves it is a `documents:runQuery` against the REST API. A listener's cutoff is fixed when its query is built, so matches age into the results and never back out of them; re-filtering on a timer is the whole reason `open_matches.ts` holds one. That window is measured on the browser's clock against a stamp written on the server's, and Firestore offers no offset between them, so a clock more than five minutes fast removes the card as completely as a missing index does and logs nothing at all. The lobby also asks for the player's identity *before* it asks for the matches, and the order is load-bearing rather than incidental: reaching for auth is what introduces the signed-in user to the database client, and this query is the one read in the app not already behind a sign-in. `createdAt` is deliberately not part of `MatchState`: a server timestamp is null in the writer's own cache until the server sets it, and a match that would not parse without one is a match its own creator could not read.

- **Emotes**: What a player says to the table is six glyphs and a five-second wait, and it is the one thing in a match no rule reads. `src/match/emotes.ts` holds the set, and what crosses the network is a position in it, exactly as a colour is a position in `die_skins.ts` — carried, shown, and never judged. It crosses in a subcollection of its own, `matches/{code}/emotes/{uid}`, one document per player and overwritten every time they send, and both halves of that are load-bearing. A subcollection rather than a field, because the match document carries the bowl and is written by two transactions that read it first: an emote landing between one of those reads and its commit makes the transaction retry — harmlessly, since it re-reads and every guard still holds, but on the hottest write path in the game. A slot rather than an append, because a slot can never grow past the seats at the table, so nothing is ever left behind to sweep up, and because it answers simultaneity for free — a whole table reacting to one bowl is writing to as many different documents as it has players, and there is nothing to serialise or to limit. What that costs is a shared order: every client shows emotes as they arrive, so two of them can show two near-simultaneous emotes the other way round, and nothing anywhere turns on the answer. Beside the emote the document carries a `sentAt` that nothing reads and nothing may — it is written by the server clock and is null in the sender's own cache until the server answers — and it is not decoration: overwriting a slot with the same emote twice running would otherwise put back a document identical to the one already there, and an unchanged document is not a change a listener is obliged to report. What it costs is the other half of the same coin: a stamp is a value the server fills in, so every write this browser makes is delivered to its own listener twice — once out of the cache with the stamp still empty, and again with what the server put there — and both parse to the same emote. So the listener drops any delivery still carrying a pending write. Measured on the document rather than on the seat, because the seat stopped being this browser's to recognise the moment it began speaking for the bots: their emotes are written under them, which is what puts them through the listener like anybody else's and is exactly what the seat filter no longer catches. The throws beneath the match are kept out of the same trap by counting sequence numbers instead. The listener is primed exactly as the throws above it are, off the first delivery the server confirms, or a player arriving part way through a match is shown every seat's last emote at once as though the whole table had just spoken. Its own failure is logged and never put on screen: a match that has lost its chatter is still a match, and that error line is never cleared. That does mean a store which has never been told this collection exists fails in complete silence, and the console is the only place it is ever said — the same trap the open matches keep, and worth checking rather than trusting. The wait between one emote and the next is the interface declining to offer a second rather than the store refusing one, like every other rule in this game, and it is deliberately not kept across a reload, since reloading a match costs a great deal more than five seconds.

  A bot speaks too, and answers the bowl rather than a clock — `nextBotEmote` in `bots.ts`, asked of every bot at the table the moment a verdict has finished playing out. That moment is the rule rather than a detail: a verdict lands before its dice have been taken out, so a bot remarking then would be talking over the thing it is remarking on. Two of the things it can say are about a hand rather than about the bowl — somebody thrown down to nothing, and itself down to one die — and both are measured against the seat that threw, because a hand only ever shrinks on its own player's throw. That is what lets the change be read off the match as it now stands with no memory of how it stood before, which is the same trick `judged` plays with the verdict. Every answer is then put to a draw whose odds rise with how much just happened, and only one bot speaks per throw, drawn from those not still quiet from the last thing they said: a whole table answering one flush at once is a wall of glyphs rather than a table talking, and it would spend the log's four rows on a single moment. Like every other move made for a bot it is done by the view holding the bot seats alone, and the write is named after the bot rather than after the browser — which is what puts it through the same listener as anybody else's, and why it is the one emote this screen does not also show for itself. A refused one is dropped in silence, since nobody pressed anything.

  The tray of glyphs is put away by the next press that lands outside it, and that press is deliberately let through to whatever it was aimed at, the way the lobby's colours are — which is what makes going from the tray straight to Pass one press rather than two. On this screen that costs something the lobby's does not: a press falling through to the canvas is an aim, and two of them inside `DROP_DOUBLE_PRESS_TIME` is `drop()` and the player's whole hand in the bowl, unasked. So `canThrow` is shut for as long as the tray is open, which closes the drag and the drop together and never even records a press for a later pair. What is drawn instead of a word is the wait itself, around the button's own rim, and it is the one place the global reduced-motion rule has to be argued with rather than obeyed: crushed to an instant, the arc empties in a hundredth of a second and leaves a button that looks live for the five seconds it is not, so under that rule it is held full and static.

## Physics

- **Scene units**: One world unit is one die width. Dice modelled at true metric scale are far below a physics engine's default tolerances, which is what makes small fast bodies jitter, sink and tunnel. Every measurement lives in `src/scene/dimensions.ts` — add new ones there rather than inline. Because the scale is not metric, `LENGTH_UNIT` has to be handed to Rapier's `integrationParameters.lengthUnit`, which sizes the engine's tolerances in metres. Almost nothing it scales matters — the overlap it leaves uncorrected and the sleep threshold both change no result in the bowl — and the one that does, the contact prediction distance, it puts far too high: at `LENGTH_UNIT` it reaches two fifths of a die width, so dice in the bowl are solved against each other before they touch and an arriving die's impact is bled away before it lands. `CONTACT_PREDICTION_DISTANCE` sets it back to twice Rapier's own default, which is enough for a die crossing most of its own width per step and not enough to deaden the bowl.
- **A throw nobody aimed**: `ThrowController.throwUnaimed` draws the two ends of a gesture instead of being handed them, and every measurement it draws from is in `dimensions.ts` beside the aimed ones. Two of the three are about keeping the dice in the bowl rather than about variety: the landing point is drawn well inside the interior floor and straight off the radius rather than off its square root, so the draw crowds toward the middle instead of spreading evenly over the disc, where a first bounce is into the rising wall. The third is the one that is not obvious. `liftLaunch` carries the launch back along the camera's own ray as it climbs, so which side of the bowl a line is drawn from decides whether the lift steepens it or flattens it — a line drawn from the camera's side is pushed further out as it rises and arrives flatter than the descent angle it asked for, flat enough with the camera orbited down to meet the outside of the near wall rather than clear the bead. So the bearing is taken from the camera's own azimuth and thrown from the far side, within `UNAIMED_BEARING_SPREAD` either way. A hand throwing badly into the same corner sees the preview line stop against the rim and aims again; nothing draws a preview for this one.
- **A drop rather than a throw**: A double press on the canvas lets the hand go straight above the point pressed on, at rest, and gravity does the rest — `ThrowController.buildDrop`. It exists beside the drag because the two gestures buy different things: a drag is aimed and can miss, and a drop cannot miss on the way in but is let go at twice the bowl's own height, arrives at some fifty-five units a second and scatters the bowl about twice as hard as an aimed throw lands. Neither is free. Three things about it are deliberate. The double press is measured at the press, from `DROP_DOUBLE_PRESS_TIME` and `DROP_DOUBLE_PRESS_SLOP`, rather than taken from the browser's `dblclick`: that event arrives only after the second release, by which time the release has already thrown the drag it was part of, and the canvas takes `touch-action: none`, which leaves a double tap synthesising a `dblclick` or not according to the browser. A drop pointed past `DROP_MAX_RADIUS` is refused rather than clamped, because a drag that runs long still describes the throw the hand meant and a drop lands where it was pointed and nowhere else. That bound is the bowl's mouth — `BOWL_MOUTH_RADIUS`, the bead's inside edge, less the die's half-diagonal — rather than the inside base, because a drop is released above the bead and falls straight down: what it has to clear is the way in, not the part of the base it would otherwise have had to land on. Inside the mouth a drop always happens, and it is the cloud that gives way rather than the gesture: a hand wide enough to hang one of its own dice over the bead is walked back toward the axis until every die of it is over the mouth, so the dice land nearer the middle than they were pointed and never a die fewer. A hand of one has no cloud and is never moved, so an ordinary turn drops exactly where it was pressed anywhere inside the bowl. And a dropped hand packs into a cloud rather than the fan a thrown hand spreads into — `cloudOffset`, filling a lattice at `DROP_CLOUD_SPACING` outwards from the middle, spaced a die's own diagonal apart so that no two of them can start overlapping whatever attitude each was drawn in. The cloud is raised by its own depth so the lowest die always falls from `DROP_HEIGHT` above the table whatever the size of the hand, and at that height it is released above the frame at most camera angles and falls into it, and it is deliberately not held under the camera the way an aimed launch is: that clamp is there because a launch above the camera lies behind it along the ray the lift walks back up, and a drop walks no ray. What that costs is aim rather than dice: the larger the hand, the nearer the axis its cloud is walked before it is let go, from the whole mouth at a hand of one or two down to half a die width off the axis at thirty-six.
- **A bowl that never settles**: The scene declares a throw over after `SETTLE_TIMEOUT` whether or not the dice agreed, because a bowl that never settles would otherwise hold the turn for good. What is read off it a moment later is published as the match's own account of the throw, so on that path alone every die still in the bowl is first forced to a rest: stopped where it is and turned onto the nearest attitude it could be resting in, which is `restingRotation` in `die_state.ts` and is built to put the die down on the face it was already showing rather than to choose a new one. Without it the value published is whichever face happened to be uppermost mid-tumble — a value nobody at the table ever saw, and one every other player then sets their own die from.
- **Contact materials**: Every collider in the scene asks for `CoefficientCombineRule.Multiply` on both friction and restitution, and anything added to the world has to as well. A pair that disagrees is resolved by a fixed precedence rather than by what either collider meant. Multiplying is what lets the die's three pairings be set apart — bouncy against another die, dull against the bowl, dead against the felt — which under Rapier's default average would be three demands on one number. What it costs is that a surface meant to grip or bounce harder than a die does can only say so by giving its own figure up to 1.0, since the product can never exceed either half; `BOWL_FRICTION` is the one that has had to.
- **What moves a struck die**: The bowl's floor, not the die that hit it. A die is tipped by the drag under its own base, so `BOWL_FRICTION` carries that number and `DIE_FRICTION` is deliberately the loosest pairing in the scene — gripping two dice together spends the throw on the die it clipped and pushes the struck one away instead of driving it down into the floor that could turn it. `DIE_RESTITUTION` is the other half: a die pinned by twenty times real gravity cannot roll without lifting its own centre of mass, so the two have to bounce apart hard enough to give it the moment.
