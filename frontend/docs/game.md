# Game

## Match

- **Match authority**: Every client is trusted; there is no server. The player whose turn it is simulates their own throw, and what they write when the dice stop is authoritative — every other player teleports their bodies onto it, which is what stops two simulations drifting apart over a match. `Die.teleport` is the one deliberate exception to the rule that only the body writes to the meshes; nothing else may write to a body from outside the scene. Nothing may write a throw the scene cannot run, either: the controls stay shut until `DishScene` reports its physics world started, because a throw is written to the match before its dice come to rest and a scene with no world never brings them to rest — the hand is charged and the turn moves nowhere. And a throw is shown here before it is written, so a write that is refused takes its dice back out again; left standing they are dice no other player has, and the retry is given the same sequence number and so the very same identifiers.
- **Reading a stored match**: `parseMatchState` is the boundary, and everything it reads is refused rather than repaired. Anything that becomes a length or a body count is bounded against `MATCH_DICE` — the dice the match was dealt plus the opening die, which no hand paid for and which is what lets a winning hand be one larger than the deal — and every stored attitude has to be a unit quaternion, because Rapier builds a body from one that is not and then never moves it or puts it to sleep, which holds the whole table unsettled. `verdict` is read as strictly as the bowl beside it despite being the replay script: `judged` on the screen, and the idempotency guards in both `submitVerdict` and the takeover, all read it as the record of what has been judged, so a verdict that will not parse would stop every turn and let the next takeover pay a throw's winnings twice.
- **Rules of play**: The whole ruleset lives in `src/match/rules.ts` as pure functions — add rules there, not in the client or the screen. A settled bowl is judged once, by whoever gets there first: the thrower does it as soon as their dice stop, and if they leave without managing it the next player judges it from their own simulation after a timeout. `MatchClient.submitVerdict` is that one write, and it is a transaction because more than one player can attempt it. It carries the whole outcome at once — the bowl, every hand, whose turn it is, and the winner — plus a `verdict` replay script every player animates from. `bowlVersion` still counts writes to the bowl and is what tells a client the verdict is new.
- **Judging a bowl**: One settled bowl is read in a fixed order, and the order is the rule rather than an implementation detail: the sixes leave the match first, and only what is left is looked at — first for a flush, then for groups. So two sixes are not a pair, and a bowl reading one to five beside a six is still a flush, because by the time the flush is asked about the six is already out of the match. A flush is the whole bowl holding one of every value a die keeps — one 1, one 2, one 3, one 4, one 5 and nothing else — and the whole of it goes back to the hand that completed it. It can never coexist with a group, since five different values share nothing, which is why the two are asked in turn rather than combined. It ends the turn exactly as a group does, and it empties the bowl, so the next player arrives at an empty one and goes all in. In the `verdict` replay a flush travels as ordinary `returned` dice: the scene animates it as it animates a pair, and nothing about the stored shape changes.
- **Hands and elimination**: A die in the bowl belongs to nobody, so `players` carries identity alone, settled for good the moment the match starts, and a hand in `pools` is what its player is holding rather than who is owed it. It is a list all the same, and not a count: each entry is one die's colour, and `poolSize` is that list's length. An empty hand *is* elimination and needs no flag: nothing in the game can take a hand off empty, since it only grows by its own player pairing dice, which needs their turn, which is skipped.
- **Colours**: A colour is paint on a die and nothing else. Every player picks one in the lobby, off the fixed palette in `src/scene/die_skins.ts`, and their six start in it; from then on the paint travels with the die rather than with the hand, so a pair won off somebody arrives in their colour and is thrown on in it. That is the whole reason a hand is a list — a count could say how much a player holds and never what. Nothing in `rules.ts` reads a colour: it is carried, handed on, and never judged. Which die a mixed hand throws is decided by the queue, oldest first, because no rule turns on the answer and a player asked to pick paint mid-turn is being asked a question with no stakes.

  The palette is fixed rather than free because every swatch answers three things at the point it is drawn: it reads against the walnut and the baize, it stays clear of the ember and green a verdict washes a die in, and it carries its own pip colour — black pips on a claret die is a face nobody can count. A wash replaces the body outright and takes the pips to dark with it, since both wash colours are light. The one die nobody threw, the opening die, is bone, which is also the colour a player is given before they choose one; the two are deliberately the same, so no reserved "nobody" value has to exist. Two people may sit down in the same colour — a colour is a skin they chose for themselves, not a badge the match hands out — but bots are drawn apart from each other and from the player, since nobody chose theirs and the draw is the only thing that can make that table readable.
- **Turn order**: The order of play is drawn at random, once, in the same write that fills the last seat and starts the match — `shuffledPlayers` in `rules.ts`, run by whoever takes that seat, like every other authority in the game. Drawing a seat as each player arrives would work just as evenly, but a player would learn their own place while the lobby was still filling, and the whole table is meant to find out at the same moment. Until the match starts, `players` is join order, which is what lets `open_matches.ts` name a lobby row after `players[0]`; from the start of the match that entry is merely whoever the draw put first.
- **Bots**: A match started with bots is an ordinary match, written straight into the `playing` phase. `MatchClient.create` is given the seats `createBots` drew — each one a `MatchPlayer` carrying `bot: true`, named from a fixed pool of six, and coloured from the eight in `die_skins.ts`, both drawn without replacement so no two bots at a table share either and neither takes what the player is using — and, since those seats fill the match on the spot, the same write does the work the last player to arrive would otherwise do: the opening die placed, the order of play drawn, the bowl counted once. Nothing has to say that no human may join, because nothing lets one: `join` refuses any match not in the `lobby` phase, and the lobby lists that phase alone, so a bot match is unlisted and unjoinable for free. What it costs instead is that one browser is now playing several seats, which is why `submitThrow` and `submitVerdict` are told which seat threw rather than assuming the browser's own. The second matters more than it looks: the contested branch in `submitVerdict` hands the turn on for a thrower who never judged their own bowl, and measured against the browser it would fire on every bot turn and take away the one choice a bot has. A bot match lives or dies with that tab, and the ~2 seconds a bot's dice are in the air is a window where closing it strands the match for good — the throw is written, the verdict never is, and a reopened tab skips throws that were already in the match when it arrived. The window is small, only one person can reach it, and the answer is to start another match.
- **Bot strategy**: `nextBotMove` in `bots.ts`, and it is a smaller decision than it sounds. A player may only pass once they have thrown, so the first throw of every turn is forced and the only choice a bot has is whether to throw *again* after one that paired nothing. What it decides against is the bowl, and the bowl is never large: every verdict takes out the sixes and then every group, so a bowl at rest holds distinct values from one to five and never all five — four dice is its ceiling. That makes one throw countable. With B dice in the bowl the thrown die is a six and leaves the match (one in six), matches one already there and brings the pair back (B in six), completes a flush and brings the whole bowl back (at four only, one in six), or settles in beside the others. In dice to the hand that is -0.67 at one, -0.33 at two, level at three and +1.17 at four, so the rule is to throw again at three or four and to pass under that. Three is taken rather than declined even though it is level on its own: a die that settles walks the bowl up to four, where the next throw is strongly in the thrower's favour and the turn is still theirs. The one exception is a hand of one, where a throw that pairs nothing is elimination and there is no way back off zero — that die is only risked on a bowl of four, where five outcomes in six pay. The count ignores what the arriving die does to the dice already in the bowl, which can turn a face and is not worth modelling; it cuts both ways.
- **Open matches**: The lobby lists every match still in the `lobby` phase and made less than five minutes ago, and a row pressed takes the same seat the code field takes. Every match is listed, so a code is a convenience rather than a gate — a stranger can sit down in one without ever being told it. This is the one query in the game that reads the `matches` collection rather than a document by name, and it needs two things nothing else does: an `allow list` rule on `/matches/{code}`, and a composite index on `phase` and `createdAt`. The index is declared in `../backend/firestore.indexes.json` and is the one thing this repo says about Firestore itself; the rules are still console-only and are not reproducible from here. Without either, the listener fails and the card simply never appears, which is why no player is ever shown a word about it. The error goes to the console rather than the screen, though that is worth checking rather than trusting — a missing index has been observed reaching the browser with nothing logged at all, and the query that proves it is a `documents:runQuery` against the REST API. A listener's cutoff is fixed when its query is built, so matches age into the results and never back out of them; re-filtering on a timer is the whole reason `open_matches.ts` holds one. That window is measured on the browser's clock against a stamp written on the server's, and Firestore offers no offset between them, so a clock more than five minutes fast removes the card as completely as a missing index does and logs nothing at all. The lobby also asks for the player's identity *before* it asks for the matches, and the order is load-bearing rather than incidental: reaching for auth is what introduces the signed-in user to the database client, and this query is the one read in the app not already behind a sign-in. `createdAt` is deliberately not part of `MatchState`: a server timestamp is null in the writer's own cache until the server sets it, and a match that would not parse without one is a match its own creator could not read.

## Physics

- **Scene units**: One world unit is one die width. Dice modelled at true metric scale are far below a physics engine's default tolerances, which is what makes small fast bodies jitter, sink and tunnel. Every measurement lives in `src/scene/dimensions.ts` — add new ones there rather than inline. Because the scale is not metric, `LENGTH_UNIT` has to be handed to Rapier's `integrationParameters.lengthUnit`, which sizes the engine's tolerances in metres. Almost nothing it scales matters — the overlap it leaves uncorrected and the sleep threshold both change no result in the bowl — and the one that does, the contact prediction distance, it puts far too high: at `LENGTH_UNIT` it reaches two fifths of a die width, so dice in the bowl are solved against each other before they touch and an arriving die's impact is bled away before it lands. `CONTACT_PREDICTION_DISTANCE` sets it back to twice Rapier's own default, which is enough for a die crossing most of its own width per step and not enough to deaden the bowl.
- **A throw nobody aimed**: `ThrowController.throwUnaimed` draws the two ends of a gesture instead of being handed them, and every measurement it draws from is in `dimensions.ts` beside the aimed ones. Two of the three are about keeping the dice in the bowl rather than about variety: the landing point is drawn well inside the interior floor and straight off the radius rather than off its square root, so the draw crowds toward the middle instead of spreading evenly over the disc, where a first bounce is into the rising wall. The third is the one that is not obvious. `liftLaunch` carries the launch back along the camera's own ray as it climbs, so which side of the bowl a line is drawn from decides whether the lift steepens it or flattens it — a line drawn from the camera's side is pushed further out as it rises and arrives flatter than the descent angle it asked for, flat enough with the camera orbited down to meet the outside of the near wall rather than clear the bead. So the bearing is taken from the camera's own azimuth and thrown from the far side, within `UNAIMED_BEARING_SPREAD` either way. A hand throwing badly into the same corner sees the preview line stop against the rim and aims again; nothing draws a preview for this one.
- **Contact materials**: Every collider in the scene asks for `CoefficientCombineRule.Multiply` on both friction and restitution, and anything added to the world has to as well. A pair that disagrees is resolved by a fixed precedence rather than by what either collider meant. Multiplying is what lets the die's three pairings be set apart — bouncy against another die, dull against the bowl, dead against the felt — which under Rapier's default average would be three demands on one number. What it costs is that a surface meant to grip or bounce harder than a die does can only say so by giving its own figure up to 1.0, since the product can never exceed either half; `BOWL_FRICTION` is the one that has had to.
- **What moves a struck die**: The bowl's floor, not the die that hit it. A die is tipped by the drag under its own base, so `BOWL_FRICTION` carries that number and `DIE_FRICTION` is deliberately the loosest pairing in the scene — gripping two dice together spends the throw on the die it clipped and pushes the struck one away instead of driving it down into the floor that could turn it. `DIE_RESTITUTION` is the other half: a die pinned by twenty times real gravity cannot roll without lifting its own centre of mass, so the two have to bounce apart hard enough to give it the moment.
